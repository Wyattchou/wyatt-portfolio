01 / The challenge

A personal experience.
An unclear path forward.

Escape is an evolving self-care app that combines mood check-ins, guided wellness activities, and an AI companion. I worked across UX/UI design and UX research as the team prepared its mobile experience for broader beta feedback.

The work addressed two connected questions: could people understand and complete the core experience, and could the team collect useful evidence about what happened afterward?

A wellness experience asks people to share how they feel and act on the guidance they receive. Missing feedback, an unclear next step, or an interrupted activity can undermine confidence before someone has experienced the product's value.

How might we identify where users lose clarity or confidence—and collect feedback grounded in actual product use?
The product question

Can I complete the journey?

Understand instructions, interpret feedback, find an activity, and recover when something goes wrong.

The research question

What does the feedback mean?

Distinguish an unclear interaction from a broken build, and an informed response from a first impression.

02 / Research foundation

Direction before certainty.

I contributed to a cross-functional, five-week exploratory research effort combining surveys, interviews, and usability research. The early work explored comfort with AI, the need for guidance, and barriers to immersive wellness experiences.

One small quantitative component involved four respondents aged 18–24. I treated it as exploratory: useful for shaping subsequent questions, without making population-level claims or turning a handful of responses into persuasive percentages.

A

Guidance needs to be explicit.

Early research raised questions about how people understand the experience and identify their next action.

B

AI interest does not establish AI trust.

A positive reaction to the concept does not tell us whether people understand a real response or feel able to question it.

C

First use should reduce the burden.

Complicated onboarding risks overwhelming the wellness experience it is meant to introduce.

These themes came from shared exploratory research. They informed what to investigate next; they are not independently verified market conclusions.

03 / Making it testable

Turn a broad product
into observable tasks.

I developed a task-based testing flow in Maze around a realistic path through the app. It covered entry, AI-related feedback, discovery, a complete activity, and finding the profile.

I also reviewed TestFlight builds directly, documenting missing feedback states, navigation dead ends, and interruptions in core activities. Some issues could stop a participant before the intended task was complete.

A critical distinction

A failed task can reveal a broken build.

A frozen screen should not automatically become evidence that the navigation is confusing. I separated build-stability problems from interaction questions so a technical blocker would not contaminate the interpretation of usability results.

Without a verified results export, this planned flow does not support claims about completion rates, misclick rates, or measured improvement.

04 / Product questions

Make the next decision
more precise.

Research and build reviews informed conversations about onboarding, mood feedback, session continuity, and the AI companion's transparency. I focused on three questions.

What has the AI interpreted?

Explore whether people understand what information informed a response and how they might question or correct an interpretation. Personalization alone does not establish trust.

What should happen next?

Examine the transition from a mood check-in to an activity. People need a clear next action before they can discover the value of a guided session.

What happens when the system fails?

Evaluate loading, empty, interrupted, and recovery states as part of the core experience. A broken transition can undermine both continuity and confidence.

A teammate's onboarding recommendations explicitly drew on our team's earlier research. That is evidence of research informing shared design discussions; I do not claim to have independently authored or shipped those designs.

I also participated in reviews of emerging experiences, including AI Soundscapes and immersive visual elements, focusing on hierarchy, orientation, and how clearly the beta communicated available functionality.

05 / The feedback journey

An invitation is not
an experience.

TestFlight could distribute the app, but an invitation alone did not establish whether someone installed it, completed a session, or had enough exposure to comment on its value. Discord supported conversation, yet inconsistent engagement made it a weak single channel for structured outreach.

I helped coordinate TestFlight invitations and wrote welcome communication. Separately, I developed a proposed follow-up sequence using Brevo and Google Forms.

The timing trade-off

Initial proposal

Time-based follow-up

Simpler to launch while reliable in-app event signals were not established as available. Less precise about what each tester had experienced.

Future direction

Exposure-aware follow-up

Request feedback after a meaningful check-in, activity, or AI interaction. Requires reliable instrumentation and careful consent handling.

The plan also considered opt-outs, duplicate reminders, and avoiding requests for sensitive wellness information in public discussions. Willingness to recommend or pay remained a research question, not an achieved outcome.

06 / Contributions & limits

What I contributed.
What the evidence supports.

Shared research

Exploratory research contributions within a cross-functional team, later cited in a teammate's onboarding recommendations.

Designed & evaluated

A task-based Maze flow, firsthand TestFlight build evaluations, issue documentation, and product feedback.

Coordinated

Tester invitations and welcome communication to help participants enter the beta.

Proposed

A structured follow-up and survey process, with a path toward more relevant timing based on product exposure.

The evidence boundary

This work does not establish a verified increase in retention, onboarding completion, trust, or satisfaction. Those claims would require outcome data, a baseline, and confirmation of what shipped. The contribution was making research questions, testing prerequisites, and feedback needs more concrete.

07 / Reflection

Protect what the
feedback actually means.

This project shifted my view of UX research from collecting feedback to protecting the validity of what feedback means. A participant struggling with an unavailable activity is telling us something important, but it may not be what a conventional usability score implies.

I also learned that recruitment is only the beginning. Exposure, timing, voluntary participation, privacy, and the team's ability to act on findings determine whether outreach informs product decisions.

What I would do next

  1. Confirm core-path stability before interpreting task failures.
  2. Run a bounded usability study with recorded outcomes and qualitative notes.
  3. Connect exposure-aware feedback to reliable product events, comparing findings by build version.
Good research creates the conditions in which the answers are interpretable.
