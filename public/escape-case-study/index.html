01 / Context

The real challenge.

An AI wellness app can feel beautifully immersive—and still leave people unsure what to do next.

Escape brings mood check-ins, guided wellness activities, and an AI companion into a single mobile experience. I worked across UX/UI and UX research while the team developed and tested that experience.

The challenge had two layers. First, could users navigate the core experience and understand the system’s response to them? Second, could the team collect feedback that reflected meaningful product use rather than a first impression—or a broken build?

My contributions connected exploratory research, evaluation of TestFlight builds, structured task design, and the planning of a more deliberate beta follow-up experience. I did not independently design or ship the entire product.

Guiding question

How might we find the moments where people lose clarity or confidence—and make the resulting feedback reliable enough to guide decisions?

02 / Research foundation

Start with questions.
Not conclusions.

Shared research

I contributed to a five-week cross-functional research effort involving exploratory surveys, interviews, and usability research. Early signals pointed toward the importance of clear guidance, understandable AI behavior, and comfortable onboarding.

Early quantitative responses were drawn from a very small exploratory group; the findings were directional, not representative.
A favorable reaction to AI or immersive experiences does not prove that first-use interactions are understandable.
Research themes became hypotheses to investigate through tasks and build reviews—not claims of solved problems.
03 / Task-based evaluation

Follow the experience.

Instead of asking only whether users liked the interface, I designed tasks around things they needed to accomplish.

I developed a Maze testing flow for a realistic first-use journey. The planned sequence covered orientation, interpretation, discovery, session continuity, and navigation. It is presented here as study design, without claiming completed sessions or measured results.

Questions surfaced during evaluation

Evaluated · TestFlight builds
01

Does the system make sense?

People need to understand that a mood or AI interaction registered their input and what the next step will be. Missing feedback states make that relationship unclear.

02

Can the session continue?

Controls, timers, previews, and transitions need to behave consistently. A failed action can interrupt the intended experience and invalidate downstream test steps.

03

Can users regain orientation?

The route back to profile or another feature should be understandable without researcher intervention, especially after completing a guided activity.

The distinction that mattered

Build failure

An activity cannot advance because a control or transition is broken. The task outcome primarily identifies a technical blocker.

Flag & retest

Usability friction

The interaction works, but a person hesitates, misinterprets a label, or struggles to find the next action.

Investigate the design
I evaluated TestFlight builds and documented issues. This distinction is an interpretation framework, not a claim that every observed failure had a proven root cause.
04 / Research into design

Make the next decision clearer.

Research becomes useful when it changes the questions designers and engineers ask—not when it is presented as a decorative chart.

Shared design discussions

I used research and build observations to contribute to discussions around onboarding, mood feedback, AI transparency, session continuity, and emerging features such as AI Soundscapes.

Some of the most productive questions concerned the system's boundaries: What did the AI interpret? Can a user correct it? What happens when an activity fails? Does the interface distinguish a loading state from an unresponsive one?

Another designer's onboarding recommendations drew on earlier research from our team. That is evidence of cross-functional influence—not evidence that I authored the final onboarding design or that those proposals shipped.

05 / Beta experience

An invitation is not an insight.

Beta operations and UX research are connected—but they measure very different things.

I helped coordinate TestFlight invitations and welcome communication, and developed a separate proposal for follow-up messages and a concise feedback survey. The intended sequence moved from access to experience, then to feedback and team review.

TestFlight could distribute the app; Discord could support conversation. Neither by itself established whether a tester had completed a meaningful session. That shaped the feedback plan.

06 / Contributions & evidence

What I actually contributed.

A case study should distinguish work delivered, work proposed, and impact that has not yet been measured.

My strongest contributions were in shaping research questions, evaluating the live beta experience, documenting blockers, and planning how the team would hear from testers more consistently.

Contributed & documented

Research and usability evaluation

Exploratory team research, a structured Maze task flow, and firsthand review of beta builds, including functional issues affecting core journeys.

Coordinated

Beta invitations and welcome

TestFlight tester onboarding and initial communication to help participants understand how to start exploring the app.

Designed / proposed

Feedback operating plan

A welcome, check-in, and survey sequence—with follow-up logic, channel choices, and safeguards for a future more personalized version.

What the work established

A clearer framework for separating technical blockers from usability problems, specifying testable product journeys, and collecting feedback after more informed app exposure.

What I cannot claim yet

No verified improvement in retention, onboarding completion, satisfaction, or AI trust. Those outcomes would require comparable baseline data, confirmed shipping status, and attributable measurement.

07 / Reflection

Better evidence, better choices.

The most important shift in my thinking was learning to protect the meaning of research—not just collect more of it.

A person who cannot finish an activity because a button is broken is telling us something important, but not necessarily that the interaction design is difficult. Likewise, a beta participant who received an invitation but never explored the app cannot answer the same questions as someone who completed multiple sessions.

I learned to think about research quality as a chain: stable tasks, clear observation, participant context, careful interpretation, and a team process for acting on the results.

What I would validate next
01

Stabilize the core journey

Confirm key actions work on the target build before evaluating completion or navigation comprehension.

02

Run bounded usability sessions

Record actual task outcomes, errors, and participant reasoning, linked to build versions.

03

Connect feedback to exposure

Use appropriate, consented product events where reliable; compare feedback by meaningful activity rather than invitation alone.

The takeaway

Good research doesn't just ask better questions. It makes the answers usable.

Escape taught me that the conditions surrounding a study—product stability, timing, transparency, and collaboration—are part of the user experience too. That perspective now shapes how I approach both research and product design.

— Wyatt Chou
